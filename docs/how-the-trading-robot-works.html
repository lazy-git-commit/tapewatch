How the Trading Robot Works

A computer program that reads financial news the second it's published, decides whether it's the rare kind that moves a share price, and — if every safety check passes — buys the stock for about an hour with a safety net underneath it.

PRACTICE MODE — pretend money Trades US stocks ~£250 per trade Fully automatic Runs on a small server, 24/5

The big picture

Five steps, once a minute, all day

The whole idea in one sentence: good news makes a share price drift upward for the next half hour or so — if you can spot the genuinely good news fast enough, you can ride that drift.

Everything the robot does is one of these five steps. Most headlines die at step 2 or 3. On many days nothing gets through at all — and that's the system working, not failing.

Step 1 · Listen

Read the news wire, bin the junk

Every minute the robot pulls the newest headlines from a financial news wire. Most of what arrives is noise, and it's thrown away before the AI ever sees it — that keeps the expensive step cheap and, more importantly, keeps garbage from ever becoming a trade.

Step 2 · Read

An AI grades each headline — but the data picks what's tradeable

Surviving headlines go to an AI reader (Claude). For each one it answers three questions: what kind of news is this (drug approval? contract win? earnings?), how confident am I it's genuinely positive, and has the price already moved — because if the jump already happened, we'd be buying someone else's profit.

Here's the important part: the AI only labels the news. Which labels are allowed to trade is decided by evidence. For 60 days the robot graded every headline and then checked what the share price actually did in the hour afterwards. Step 3 · Check

Thirteen safety checks against the live price

A great headline isn't enough — the market's reaction has to look right too. Before any money moves, the stock must pass a gauntlet of checks, run in order from cheapest to most expensive. One failure and the trade is off.

The checks that matter most, in plain words

  • 01Not the opening chaosNo buying in the first 5 minutes after the market opens — prices are wild and misleading there.
  • 02Not a penny stockUnder $5 a share means manipulation risk and brutal costs. Skip.
  • 03Easy to get out ofThe stock must normally trade at least $5M a day, so our exit can't move the price against us.
  • 04Real buyers showed upToday's trading volume must be well above normal for this time of day. News without volume is news nobody believed.
  • 05Not already exhaustedIf the stock is already up 25%+ today, or just climbed all the way back from a big morning drop, the move has happened. We'd be buying the top.
  • 06Above fair value — but barelyPrice must sit above the day's volume-weighted average (the "fair value line") — proof big money is buying — but no more than 1.5% above it. Any higher and a normal dip would hit our safety net before the rise could play out.

One clever wrinkle: some failures are "not yet" rather than "no." If the only problem is that buyers haven't arrived yet, or the price is stretched a bit too high right now, the robot doesn't discard the idea — it re-checks every minute for 15 minutes and buys the dip if things come right. That's how a patient human trader behaves: don't chase, wait for the pullback.

Step 4 · Buy, protected

Small position, safety net first

A trade is deliberately small: about 5% of the pot (~£250 in practice mode), sized so that if the trade fails, the loss is a fraction of a percent of the account. The moment the buy completes, a safety-net sell order is lodged with the broker at 2% below our price. That matters: even if our server crashes or the internet dies, the broker's own computers will sell the position if it falls 2%. The downside is never unguarded.

And if the trade gets going — up 2% — the safety net is raised to the price we paid. From that moment the worst case is roughly break-even: a winning trade is no longer allowed to turn into a losing one.

Step 5 · Exit by rules

Four doors out — one of them always closes the trade

No trade is ever left to "see how it goes." Every position leaves through the first of these four doors it reaches, and nothing is ever held overnight — a bad surprise while the market is closed can't be defended against, so the robot simply never takes that risk.

Above it all

The guardrails that protect the whole account

Separate from any single trade, account-level brakes are checked before every purchase:

■

Daily kill switch

Lose 2% of the account in one day and the robot stands down until tomorrow. A bad day is never allowed to become a terrible one.

3

Max 3 positions

News clusters — one macro headline can fire five similar signals at once. The cap limits the blast radius.

10

Max 10 trades/day

A day that "needs" more than ten attempts is a day the filters are wrong.

24h

One shot per stock

After trading a stock, it's off-limits for 24 hours — follow-up articles about the same event can't re-trigger a buy.

Why it keeps improving

Every rule is on trial, every night

The robot writes down every headline it grades and every decision it makes. Each night, it looks up what the price actually did 5, 15 and 60 minutes after each headline — including all the ones it didn't trade. That scorecard is what banned four news types in Step 2, and it's how every future rule change gets decided: measure first, then change.

Where it stands

Practice mode, on purpose

The robot currently trades a demo account with pretend money. Its early record was poor — and every loss got a full post-mortem that hardened the rules above: the banned news types, the "don't chase" check, the broker-held safety net, and the break-even ratchet all came out of specific, named losing trades. That's what practice mode is for: pay for lessons in play money, keep the lessons.

The bar for switching to real money is simple and unsentimental: a sustained stretch where the measured edge is positive after costs. The nightly scorecard — not optimism — makes that call.
